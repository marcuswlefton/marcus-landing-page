David V. · Earlier engagement · Commercial performance

Give your best work
your best hours.

A commercial-performance case about the connection between capacity, work allocation, and the result.

The problem behind the goal.

The work was focused on stronger commercial performance and a working day that left room for life. In my account of the engagement, the useful question was where David’s best capacity was going, and whether the demands of the day supported the work that mattered most.

A calendar can look productive while allocating a person’s clearest thinking and attention to lower-value demands. Important commercial work then has to compete for what remains.

The judgment that shaped the work.

I looked at the match between the work and the state available to do it. Which activities deserved David’s best hours? What could move? How could recovery and a deliberate end to the day support the next day’s performance?

This is where my experience across professional sport and private advisory connects. In sport, preparation has to support the demand. In commercial work, the quality of the person’s attention and judgment matters alongside the decision about where to apply them.

What we changed.

The approach protected better hours for higher-value work, moved lower-value demands, and built recovery and a shutdown ritual into the working day. The aim was to make strong performance more repeatable while keeping the rest of life in view.

Those changes connect several dimensions: capacity to do the work, attention while doing it, execution in the calendar, and the surrounding conditions that make the next day possible.

Inside the reasoning

The working Performance Map.

A reconstruction of the approach, not an original client document. The review prompts illustrate how the work can be evaluated; they are not additional measured results.

The consequential result
Stronger, more repeatable commercial performance with a deliberate end to the working day.
The working hypothesis
Lower-value demands may be receiving the capacity that higher-value work requires.
The intervention
Protect the better hours. Move lower-value work. Plan recovery and a clear shutdown.
The review
Did the important work happen? What commercial progress followed? Did the day end where intended? What held when demand rose?

What the evidence supports.

David reported almost 200% of plan within three months and described it as his best Q1 and highest performance in five years. His quotation was previously published on this website’s proof page.

That is a client-reported commercial outcome. The published record does not independently verify the sales figures, establish how much any individual intervention contributed, or provide a quantified measure of the effect on life outside work. Recovery and a shutdown were part of the design; they should not be presented as separately proven outcomes.

What to take from the case.

When important work receives only the capacity left over, improving the fit between the person, the task, and the working day is worth examining. The right change depends on the constraint in front of you.
