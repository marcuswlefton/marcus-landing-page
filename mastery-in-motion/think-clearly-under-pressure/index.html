How to think clearly under pressure: find what is driving the reaction

Before you answer the message, understand the situation you are answering.

A message arrives: “Can we talk about the proposal?”

You read it as dissatisfaction. Your attention moves to everything that could be wrong. You begin composing an explanation before you know what the other person wants to discuss.

The message is short. The situation taking shape in your mind is much larger.

This illustrative example captures a pattern worth noticing in consequential work. An interpretation can start directing your behavior before you have examined it.

Pressure changes the conditions for judgment.

Research reviewed by neuroscientist Amy Arnsten describes how acute, uncontrollable stress can impair prefrontal functions involved in working memory and flexible thinking. That helps explain why a person may have less access to their usual perspective under pressure. It does not tell us the cause of any individual's reaction.

In advisory, I want to understand the interaction: what happened, what you made it mean, the state you brought to it, and what the decision actually requires.

The aim is to make a response that fits the facts and the stakes. Sometimes that means acting quickly. Sometimes it means asking a better question before committing.

Separate the fact from the story.

Before responding to an ambiguous message, write two short lines:

  • What I know: the person wants to discuss the proposal.
  • What I am assuming: they dislike it, the relationship is at risk, and I need to defend it now.

Then ask what evidence supports the assumption and what information would change it. The concern may be justified. The exercise gives you a way to examine it.

In this example, the most useful next move could be: “Of course. Which part would you like to discuss so I can come prepared?” That question improves the information available before you invest in a response.

Look beyond the immediate thought.

If a reaction keeps repeating, the interpretation is only one place to look. Ask what else is contributing.

  • Capacity: are you entering these conversations already depleted?
  • Cognition: which assumptions are narrowing what you notice?
  • Capability: is this a conversation or decision you need more practice handling?
  • Execution: is there a clear next action, or are several unresolved tasks competing for attention?
  • Leverage: does this actually require your involvement?
  • Context: are the stakes and response time clear, or are you filling in the gaps?

A difficult negotiation may call for preparation. Repeated false urgency may call for communication agreements. An overloaded week may call for changing the load. The explanation determines which intervention is worth testing.

Use a short decision reset.

For a non-emergency decision, take a brief pause if the situation allows. Put the decision into one sentence. Identify what is known, what remains uncertain, and when a response is actually needed.

Choose the smallest next action that improves the situation: clarify the request, gather a missing fact, prepare the conversation, or make the decision within an agreed boundary.

Afterward, review what happened. Was the original interpretation accurate? Did the action improve the outcome? What would have made you better prepared?

Over time, those observations can reveal a recurring pattern that is difficult to see from inside a single demanding moment.

What are you treating as a fact that is still an interpretation?

The same inquiry applies to the week around the decision. If your better judgment keeps getting spent on work that should not require it, read the calendar review for founders.

Research behind the idea

Amy F. T. Arnsten (2009), Stress signalling pathways that impair prefrontal cortex structure and function, Nature Reviews Neuroscience. The decision prompts above are an advisory practice, not a validated clinical intervention.
